6. Results

6.1 The bypass on a running gateway (E1)

Table 4 and Fig. 3 report the bypass rate, the share of attack rewordings answered with the poison while the gateway's log records that the filter did not run, across seven cache thresholds; each threshold uses all {{n_variants}} rewordings, and the shaded bands in Fig. 3 are 95% seed-bootstrap CIs. At τ = 0.85, a common default, {{w4_lazy_85}}% of lazy edits (95% CI {{w4_lazy_85_ci}}) and {{w4_all_85}}% of all rewordings were served the poison unscreened. Even at the strictest setting a product would plausibly use, 0.95, {{w4_lazy_95}}% of lazy edits still got through. Full paraphrases behave very differently: they match rarely at strict thresholds ({{w4_rewrite_95}}% at 0.95) and only become dangerous as the threshold loosens ({{w4_rewrite_65}}% at 0.65), because a genuine paraphrase moves the embedding much further than a changed capital letter does.

The practical reading is that the attacker needs no skill. The cheapest rewordings are the most effective, and no threshold value is both safe and useful: tightening τ from 0.85 to 0.95 lowers the lazy-edit bypass rate by only {{w4_lazy_drop}} points, while a stricter threshold necessarily matches fewer of the honest rephrasings that make a semantic cache worth having.

Four checks support the measurement. The exact seed text, which must always hit, was served the poison in {{w4_exact}} cases. 6.2 The order is the cause (E2)

Running the same build with the filter moved in front of the cache, and nothing else changed, gives a bypass rate of zero, which establishes that the ordering causes the bypass. That zero holds by construction, however, since in the filter-first order the filter always runs. The poison is still served for {{e2_served_pct}}% of rewordings in this order, because the filter itself lets those texts through: asked directly, it blocks {{e2_blocked_pct}}% of the rewordings, {{e2_blocked_jayavibhav}}% of those derived from jayavibhav seeds but only {{e2_blocked_deepset}}% of those from deepset, whose attacks are more conversational. This is the filter's own limit, not the cache's, and it is the reason every later result separates cache-caused leaks from filter misses.

Label matching stops the easy case. When the poison was saved with an attack label, refusing entries whose stored verdict is "attack" cuts cache-caused leaks from {{p3_base_attack}}% to {{p3_filter_attack}}%. The residue comes from seeds the filter itself labelled clean when the poison was stored, whose rewordings it would have caught.

It does not stop the realistic case. When the poison was saved as clean, its stored verdict looks like any honest entry's, and label matching barely helps ({{p3_filter_clean}}%). This is the inherent limit of trusting a verdict computed once: a verdict that was wrong when stored stays wrong.

Re-checking only risky hits is not enough. The cheap keyword label calls most real attacks harmless; only {{n_seeds_risky}} of the {{n_seeds}} seeds receive a risky label, and most are labelled "informational" or "other". Most poisoned hits are therefore never re-checked, and {{p3_both_clean}}% still get through with both mechanisms on.

Re-checking every hit closes the gap. With every hit screened again before it is served, cache-caused leaks fall to zero in both cases (0 of {{n_variants}}; 95% upper bound {{p3_all_upper}}%). What is still served, about {{p3_all_clean_miss}}%, is exactly the share the filter misses when asked directly, so the cache adds no risk beyond the filter's own. Honest traffic kept {{p3_all_hits}}% of its cache hits in every setting, and no honest control was served the poison in any setting.

The price is time. A re-checked hit takes about {{p3_all_hit_ms}} ms at the median, against {{p3_base_hit_ms}} ms for an unscreened hit, because it pays for one filter call. Against a real model answer, measured at a median of {{r_cheap_ms}} ms for the cheap model and {{r_strong_ms}} ms for the strong one in E5, a re-checked hit remains more than an order of magnitude faster and still costs nothing.
