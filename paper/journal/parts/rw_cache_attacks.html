Caches have long been an attack surface on the web. In web cache poisoning, a request input that changes the response but is not part of the cache key lets one attacker request store a harmful response that is then served to everyone (Kettle, 2018); cache-poisoned denial of service stores error pages the same way (Nguyen et al., 2019), and web cache deception tricks a cache into storing private pages (Gil, 2017; Mirheidari et al., 2020). The flaw we study has the same structure: the safety verdict changes whether a response may be served, but it is not part of the semantic cache's key. Verdict-tagged caching is, in these terms, the fix of keying on the input that matters.

LLM caches have recently attracted the same scrutiny. Timing side channels in shared prompt, KV and semantic caches leak other users' prompts (Zheng et al., 2024; Gu et al., 2025; Wu, Zhang, et al., 2025; Song et al., 2025). Closest to our work, Wu, Wang, et al. (2026) are the first to show that semantic caches can be poisoned so that other users receive attacker-chosen answers, on the managed caches of three cloud providers. They also test prompt-injection classifiers, including the ProtectAI model we use, as a defence against the poisoning prompts themselves, and find them weak because the poison prompts look benign; this supports the "saved as clean" case in our threat model. Wu, Ying, et al. (2026) catalogue six cache attacks on LLM serving frameworks, including fuzzy poisoning of GPTCache and a hash collision through which harmful content inherits a benign cached moderation result. Zhang, Liu, et al. (2026) mount black-box key-collision attacks on semantic caches that hijack responses, and Zhang, Yao, et al. (2026) and Liang et al. (2026) defend by checking every candidate hit for whether the cached answer is valid for the new query.
